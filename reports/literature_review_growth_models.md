# Growth Models in Secondary Mathematics: A Literature Review

**Scope:** linear, exponential, logarithmic and logistic models in Grade 11, with attention to learning and development.

## 1. Introduction

Growth models sit at the point where algebra, modelling and real-world data meet. Students meet them first as formulas and graphs, but the research suggests that understanding them requires a different kind of thinking. Two strands run through the literature:

1. **How students come to understand exponential and related functions.** Mostly from mathematics education research, with a strong role for covariational and quantitative reasoning.
2. **How growth models are taught through modelling and technology.** Mostly teaching proposals and dissertations, with the logistic model often presented as the natural next step after the exponential.

A third, separate strand comes from psychology and economics: people systematically misjudge exponential growth, whatever their schooling.

This review organises the sources by theme, then compares the underlying paradigms in tables.

## 2. How the sources were gathered

- Web searches in English and Portuguese on teaching and learning growth models, covariational reasoning, logistic modelling and exponential growth bias.
- Sources include peer-reviewed articles, conference papers, theses and dissertations, teacher-training texts and classroom resources.
- **Limitations:** no systematic database search (ERIC, Scopus, RCAAP) was run, the full texts were not read, and Brazilian sources are over-represented relative to Portuguese ones. Treat this as a map to follow up, not an exhaustive review.

## 4. Thematic review

### 4.1 Theme A: Cognitive foundations of exponential growth

Confrey and Smith (1995) argue that exponentials are usually taught as formulas tied to exponent rules and routine algorithms, and propose instead an approach based on **splitting**, a multiplicative operation that is not repeated addition. They pair it with a **covariation** view of functions, in which two quantities change together.

Castillo-Garsow (2013) extends this to high school, with a teaching experiment that moved from exponential growth to the logistic differential equation. He reports that mathematicians routinely use several, sometimes contradictory, ways of thinking about exponential growth, and warns that these are often not made distinct for students. He also notes that there is little research and only superficial agreement on how exponential growth should be taught initially.

Ellis et al. (2013) report a small teaching experiment where students explored exponentials through two continuously covarying quantities (height and time). They identify two conceptual paths: the **covariation view** and the **correspondence view**. O'Bryan's dissertation (Arizona State) examines the meanings students build for exponential functions, percent change and growth factors, within a quantitative-reasoning framework.

Carlson et al. (2003) provide a framework of mental actions for covariational reasoning that other studies use as an analytical lens.

**What the theme agrees on:** understanding exponential growth is more than recognising a formula, and it develops through reasoning about how quantities change together.

### 4.2 Theme B: Misconceptions and levels of understanding

Bergsma (2019) studied 17 secondary students in the Netherlands, using a survey and semi-structured interviews. Students at lower levels coped with positive integer bases and powers but struggled with negative bases or powers. Notable errors included moving the minus sign of a power onto the base, not being able to explain why exponent rules hold, and composing growth factors incorrectly when given two coordinates.

The Educare review on the logistic equation concludes that difficulties centre on **multiple representations** (especially the equation viewed as a differential equation) and on **interdisciplinary communication**. It notes that the proposals in the literature use varied approaches, technologies and theoretical frameworks.

### 4.3 Theme C: Modelling as a teaching approach

This is the dominant approach in the Portuguese-language sources.

- The **SPM 2018 teacher-training text** treats Malthus's exponential and Verhulst's logistic models as part of the modelling topics in the Portuguese *Programa e Metas Curriculares de Matemática A*. ⚠ Author not identified.
- **Narcizo (2016)** used modelling activities with a second-year high-school class. Students discovered exponential models they did not yet know and reached the compound-interest formula. The first activities used affine (linear) functions, so the study contrasts linear with exponential.
- **Lima's teaching sequence** proposes contextualised teaching of exponential functions through modelling, including pharmacokinetic decay.
- **Santos (2021)** targets upper-secondary classrooms with growth and epidemiological models, linking exponential growth to the spread of viruses.
- **Siller et al. (2023)** frame exponential growth as a "rich learning environment" for modelling. ⚠ Seen only in a citing list.
- A **study in a Serbian school** (*Open Schools Journal*) uses linear and exponential models with data fitting in a real-world context. ⚠ Author and year not captured.

**Common pattern:** start from a real situation, collect or receive data, fit a model (often with GeoGebra or a spreadsheet), and interpret the parameters. Most are design proposals or small classroom studies rather than large-scale evaluations.

### 4.4 Theme D: The logistic model

Several sources treat the logistic model as the natural extension of the exponential, adding a capacity limit.

- The **Vensim dissertation (UFSJ)** argues that most real growth processes have limited capacity and are therefore logistic. It proposes teaching logistic growth through recurrence relations, Excel and causal-loop diagrams, avoiding differential equations.
- **Wasson (2021)** tested pictorial diagrams with university biology students. They did not significantly improve mastery of exponential and logistic models, though predator-prey diagrams helped students build new models.
- **Aronhime (2013)** proposes an activity module with pre- and post-assessments, hands-on counting data and semi-log graphs, because these concepts are conceptually challenging in traditional lectures.
- **Classroom resources** (Concord Consortium, K20 Center, Texas Instruments) use simulations such as outbreaks and population curves to contrast exponential with logistic growth.

**Gap:** the logistic model has less empirical research with secondary students than the exponential. Most of what exists is university-level or proposal-based.

### 4.5 Theme E: The logarithmic model and the exponential–logarithm link

The literature is thinner here, and the Ferrari paper says so directly: little has been researched and reported on the logarithm function, and many writings are class notes or suggestions rather than studies of how students come to understand it.

- **Trejo, Ferrari & Martínez (2021)** study logarithmic–exponential covariational reasoning in pre-service teachers and note how hard it is to develop continuous covariation reasoning from a task that encourages discrete covariation.
- **Ferrari** proposes that covarying arithmetic and geometric progressions can support a more robust notion of the logarithm.
- **Borges (2014)** uses progressions and mathematical models to teach logarithms and exponentials, and suggests studying arithmetic and geometric progressions first.
- **Pinheiro (2021)** and **Alves (2014)** offer conceptual and graphical treatments, with Alves also examining how the functions appear in secondary textbooks.
- **Tavoni & Oliveira (2022)** use Malthus and Verhulst to motivate logarithms and exponentials, noting that these functions are a major obstacle for students and teachers.

### 4.6 Theme F: Exponential growth bias

This strand comes from psychology and economics and is relevant because it explains why the topic is hard, independent of schooling.

- **Timmers & Wagenaar (1977)** found that exponential growth shown as numbers or graphs is grossly underestimated, and that the misperception lessened with decreasing functions.
- **Schonger & Sele (2021)** found the bias is much smaller when doubling times are used, and that it emerges even among a highly educated sample aware of the bias.
- **Melnik-Leroy et al. (2023)** found that a log scale led to errors in describing graphs, while a linear scale misled predictions. A short intervention reduced the difficulties, with a stronger learning effect for participants with a better mathematical education.
- **Levy & Tasoff** and **Levy** report the bias in a financial context and that people are overconfident when answering exponential questions.
- **McKenzie & Liersch** found that highlighting the exponential growth of savings changed saving intentions.
- A **critical re-analysis** of Wagenaar & Sagaria (1975) (PMC9169707) argues the classic study had flaws, including a misspecified growth process. ⚠ Authors not captured.

**Relevance to Grade 11:** the findings on framing (doubling times) and on graph scales bear directly on how to present problems, and on teaching the logarithmic scale.

### 4.7 Theme G: Technology and systems dynamics

- **Vensim** (causal-loop diagrams) and **spreadsheets** make the logistic model accessible through recurrence rather than calculus.
- **GeoGebra** is used in the Darlis Silva product for fitting models and linearising data, which brings in logarithms.
- **TI calculators, Desmos and MATLAB** appear in classroom resources.

## 6. Synthesis

**Agreements**
- Exponential growth is hard, and formulas alone do not build understanding.
- Real-world contexts and data are widely used to motivate the models.
- The logistic model follows naturally from the exponential and can be introduced without a full differential-equations course.

**Tensions**
- **Several ways of thinking at once.** Castillo-Garsow warns that using different views of exponential growth without distinguishing them can confuse students, while modelling proposals often move between views freely.
- **Scale choice.** The log scale can reduce bias in some studies and increase errors in others, so how to introduce logarithmic scales is open.
- **Visual aids.** Wasson found pictorial models did not improve calculation-oriented mastery, even though the diagrams helped model-building.

**Gaps**
1. Few studies follow Grade 11 students through all four models.
2. The logarithmic model is rarely studied as a growth model, mostly as an inverse function.
3. Evidence for the logistic model in secondary school is mostly proposals, not evaluations.
4. Little Portuguese empirical research was found in these searches, though a systematic search of RCAAP is likely to change that.
5. Links between growth models and learning or development as a *subject* of modelling (as in the Vensim dissertation) are underexplored.

## 7. Implications for the thesis

[To be completed once the thesis topic is known.] Possible directions that follow from the gaps above:
- A teaching sequence that moves linear → exponential → logarithmic → logistic, using covariational tasks.
- Problem framing that uses doubling times, following Schonger & Sele.
- Explicit attention to graph scales, following Melnik-Leroy et al.
- Using a learning or development process as the data context for the logistic model.
